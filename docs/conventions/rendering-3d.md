# The 3D picture

`src/rendering3d/` draws the game in three dimensions through the climber's own eyes (#311), with three.js: the Icefall first, opened with `?3d` in the address, beside the 2D picture until it has caught up with it. It follows [../conventions.md](../conventions.md) → Modules and Objects, and [../guidelines.md](../guidelines.md) for what the player sees; these are its own rules.

- **The same world, another picture.** `IcefallScene` takes the `SceneWorld` the 2D `SlopeScene` takes and answers the same calls (`open`, `eachFrame`, `draw`, `setQuality`, `screenAt`, `cover`, `passage`, `tentOver`, `pixelAt`), so the page runs either. It reads the physics, the clock, the hazards and the body and holds no game state: what it owns is the picture's own, the meshes, the light, where the head is turned, the steps' phase. The simulation, the physics, the agents and the room server know nothing of it, and the page fetches its code only with `?3d`.
- **On the trail the ground is the physics' own.** The physics knows the route as a line in a plane (`Terrain`). `Trail` bends that plane into a trail that winds up the valley: a point `x` across the plane is `x` metres up the valley (towards −z), and the trail's centre is `aside(x)` to the right. Everything the physics places goes into the world through `Trail.place`. `GlacierShape` hangs the ground on the line: a row of points for every corner of it and every half metre between, carried out to each side. Within the trodden trail's width the ground is the line and nothing else, to the millimetre (`glacier-shape.test.ts` holds it there), so a climber's feet, a ladder's ends and a rope's anchors are on the ground they see. To the sides the line is let go of: a crevasse runs on, narrowing until it closes; a wall gives way to the next block; past `GlacierShape.REACH` the ground is the glacier's own.
- **A level is a place: the same every time.** The ground, the towers and the valley's walls come from the route and the level's own seed (`IcefallScene.LEVEL_SEED`) through `FieldNoise`, a pure function of the point: never from the expedition's seed, the clock or `Math.random()`. The same Icefall is built on every device and every climb.
- **Drawn in code.** No model, texture or picture is loaded ([../design.md](../design.md) → Decided in chat). The ground is meshes cut from `GlacierShape`; the towers are a few shapes cut from a block by fracture planes, drawn many times over (`SeracField`); the climbers, the ladders and the stakes are `Solid`s, simple pieces joined into one mesh each; the patterns of snow, ice and stone are drawn by shaders into textures once, when the level loads (`TextureBaker`).
- **60 frames a second on a laptop's built-in graphics** (the test machine: Intel UHD, 1920×1080). What that costs was measured before anything was built: such a card draws a million triangles with a dozen texture look-ups and a shadow look-up a pixel in 6 ms, and takes a third of a second over the same picture with its patterns worked out as noise at every pixel. So:
  - **no noise in a fragment shader**: a pattern is a look-up in a baked texture;
  - **no post-processing passes**: every surface's shader lights it, puts the air in front of it and writes the screen's colour itself (`SceneLight.GLSL`'s `toScreen`), into the canvas, with the browser's own anti-aliasing;
  - **the ground's shadows are drawn when the light or the climber has moved**, not every frame; only the small map of the climbers is (`SunShadows`);
  - **the level stays under 600,000 triangles of ground and towers** (`level-budget.test.ts`), cut into pieces up the valley so only those in view are drawn;
  - **sharpness gives before the frame rate does** (`Sharpness`): a level of `[Graphics]` is a budget of pixels, and a picture whose frames run long is drawn a step softer.
  A change that adds to a frame's cost says in its PR what the frame takes on the test machine, before and after: `data-triangles` and the frame time (README → The game in 3D).
- **One light.** `SceneLight` is the sun or the moon, the sky, the snow's own light thrown back, the haze and the climber's headlamp, as numbers every surface's shader reads, set each frame from the 2D picture's `Atmosphere`, so both pictures tell the same time and the same weather. A new material takes `light.uniforms` and `SceneLight.GLSL`; none lights itself its own way.
- **A climber is a point with legs.** The physics moves a climber as a point; `ClimberGait` gives the point steps from how far it went, a foot on the ground staying where it was put (`climber-gait.test.ts`), and `ClimberFigure` poses a body from the hips, the ankles and the wrists. The player's own figure is the body they look down at, without its head, and their eyes are its eyes. On a ladder up a wall, or on a face, a climber faces the ice; standing at a wall's foot they stand a body's depth off it.
- **First person is steered by forward and back.** The simulation hears the same eight-way walks as ever. `RouteSteering` works a push forward out into the walk it is where the climber stands, along the ground, up a ladder, up the rope, and the page's `FirstPersonSink` sends it on again each time that changes while the push is held; a push to a side turns the head; a tool goes where the climber looks (`IcefallScene.aim`). The head's turn is the picture's own, as the 2D camera's zoom is: no action, replay or room hears it.
- **The stories** (`e2e/icefall3d.spec.ts`) run on a PC, in software, on a small screen: they read what the picture says it drew from `#slope`'s data attributes (`data-view="3d"`, `data-frames`, `data-triangles`, `data-facing`; `data-busy` is the milliseconds of a frame the page's own code took, lately and at the longest, and `data-shadows` how many times the ground's shadows were drawn), and turn the head through `window.everest.look`.

Not there yet, each with its own issue to come: the tents, the hazards' pictures (a serac's collapse, an avalanche), the falls and the rescue seen in first person, snowfall and blown snow, the phones' controls, and the real peaks' shapes from survey data.
